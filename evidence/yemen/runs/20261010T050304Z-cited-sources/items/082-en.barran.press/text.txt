Yemeni Bank for Reconstruction and Development Joins Exodus to Aden, Ninth to Flee Houthi Sanctions
Monday, 14 July 2025 1 year ago
Barran Press
Yemeni Bank for Reconstruction and Development - Archive



 

The Yemeni Bank for Reconstruction and Development announced Monday, July 14, 2025, it has officially transferred its headquarters from Houthi-controlled Sana'a to Aden, the country's interim capital. This strategic move makes it the ninth bank to heed directives from the Central Bank of Yemen to relocate, aiming to shield itself from international sanctions.

In an internal circular, obtained by "Baran Press," the bank explicitly stated the relocation "comes within the framework of ensuring the bank avoids the risks of international sanctions, particularly those associated with being classified among prohibited entities or those subject to measures by the U.S. Department of the Treasury." The decision aligns with directives from the Aden-based Central Bank and international community demands, mirroring actions taken by other financial institutions recently.

The Central Bank of Yemen in Aden had previously revealed on March 16, 2025, that eight other banks had already committed to moving their main centers from Sana'a to Aden to evade stringent U.S. sanctions. These included prominent names such as Al-Tadhamon Bank, Al-Kuraimi Islamic Microfinance Bank, Yemen Bahrain Comprehensive Bank, Yemeni Islamic Bank, Saba Islamic Bank, Yemen and Gulf Bank, Yemeni Commercial Bank, and Al-Amal Microfinance Bank.

Conversely, the Houthi-controlled Central Bank branch in Sana'a has condemned these relocations, accusing the internationally recognized government of coercing banks and blaming the "Saudi regime" for perceived threats against the banking sector. The Sana'a bank claimed the transfers are designed to "target the national economy and exploit the so-called American classification to threaten the banking sector with direct incitement from their Saudi and Emirati patrons," warning that Saudi Arabia would bear the consequences of any such actions.

The U.S. executive order designating the Houthi group as a "foreign terrorist organization" became effective on March 3, 2025. This classification has significantly tightened restrictions on international transactions for banks still operating from Sana'a. Such institutions face severe repercussions, including the potential freezing of overseas accounts or disconnection from the global financial system, forcing a difficult choice: comply with the legitimate Central Bank in Aden or risk crippling U.S. sanctions.


The U.S. Treasury Department previously sanctioned the Yemen and Kuwait Bank for alleged financial involvement with the Iran-backed Houthi group. This action serves as a stark warning, indicating other banks could face similar punitive measures if they continue operations from Houthi-controlled territories.
